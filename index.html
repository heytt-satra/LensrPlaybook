Built from $100M Offers value equation: Dream Outcome x Perceived Likelihood / Effort x Time Delay. Every package below is structured to maximise the top and minimise the bottom of that equation.

Dream outcome: A shoot that runs perfectly with zero vendor coordination, cinema-quality output, on time, every time.
Perceived likelihood: BFC official partner. BeerBiceps Season 1. BTDT x Snitch. The Founder and The Funder every episode. NLEY Consultants ongoing. Old Monk VFX Studio recurring.
Effort and sacrifice removed: One call. One invoice. Everything confirmed.
Time delay removed: 24/7 availability. Short notice is normal.

The grand slam guarantee
Hormozi principle: "Guarantees allow customers to try your stuff risk-free." Lensr guarantee framing (use this verbally and in proposals): "If we show up and anything in the kit is not what was confirmed, we replace it on the same day at no additional cost. If we cannot, you do not pay for that item. Simple." This removes the biggest fear any client has: paying for gear that does not show up right. Most rental houses do not offer this. Stating it out loud closes more deals than any discount will.

Cold call script

Hormozi's cold call framework: get permission to pitch, make them feel understood, ask a diagnostic question, and let their answer do the selling. You are not closing on the first call. You are opening a conversation.

Opening (first 8 seconds)
Hey [Name], this is [Your name] from Lensr. We handle camera gear and production crew for shoots in Mumbai. Did I catch you at a bad time? [Wait for answer. If yes: "No problem, when is a better time to call?" If no: continue.]
Permission bridge
Perfect. I will keep this short. We work with agencies and brands in Mumbai that are doing regular shoot days and want the gear and crew sorted from one place instead of juggling three different vendors. I wanted to see if that is something that is relevant to what you are doing. [Wait. Let them respond. If they say yes or maybe: continue. If they say no: ask the diagnostic question anyway.]
Diagnostic question (the Hormozi move)
Can I ask, when you have a shoot day coming up, how are you currently handling the gear and crew side of it? Are you renting from a house, working with freelancers, or do you have something in-house? [Let them talk. Do not interrupt. Their answer tells you everything. Then respond to the specific gap they just described.]
If they describe the vendor juggle problem
That is exactly what we hear from most teams. The problem is every shoot becomes a new coordination project. We solve that. Objection handling

Hormozi: never fight an objection. Acknowledge it, reframe it, and redirect. Every objection is information about what the prospect actually needs to hear. The goal is not to win the argument. It is to help them see the real picture. Tap any question to expand.

"Your rates are too expensive."
I hear you. Can I ask what you are currently spending per shoot day when you factor in gear rental, DOP, gimbal operator, and audio separately? Most clients find that when they add those up, the difference is smaller than it looks. And with us, you get one confirmed invoice with no surprise add-ons on shoot day. If the number still does not work for your budget, tell me what the budget is and I will tell you honestly whether we can make it work or not. No point wasting each other's time.
Hormozi: never discount without asking a qualifying question first. Make them do the math on their current cost. The price objection usually disappears when they calculate what they are already spending.
"We already have a vendor we use."
That makes sense. Can I ask, when your regular vendor is not available or a shoot needs something they do not carry, what happens then? We work with a lot of teams in exactly that role. Not a replacement, just the backup and upgrade option when the brief calls for it. What does your current vendor not carry that has come up as a gap?
Never attack the current vendor. Ask for the gap. There is always a gap. Their answer tells you exactly how to position Lensr.
"We do not have a shoot coming up right now."
Totally fine. Most people we work with had the same answer until they had a shoot land with 48 hours notice. Can I just send you the rate card and kit list so you have it on file? Takes two minutes to scan, and when a brief comes in last minute you will know exactly who to call. What is the best email to send it to?
Convert the not-now into a warm future contact. Get the email. The rate card in their inbox is a low-friction conversion that pays off when timing changes.
"We have an in-house team."
That is great. In-house teams are the best setup for regular shoots. Two situations where most in-house teams still reach out to us: when a brief needs gear they do not own, like a drone or a cinema prime set, and when two shoots run on the same day and capacity gets stretched. Is either of those something that has come up for your team?
Validate, then plant two specific scenarios where even in-house teams need Lensr. The question at the end gets them to confirm whether the gap exists.
"Send me your rate card and I will have a look."
Absolutely. Before I do, can I ask one quick thing so I send the relevant package rather than the full list? What kind of shoots are most common for you, and roughly how often? [After they answer: "Great. I will send you the package that fits that brief rather than the whole menu. You will get exactly what is relevant and nothing you are not."
Hormozi: never send a generic PDF when you can send a personalised one. Asking this question also re-engages them in the conversation and qualifies their budget and frequency before you send anything.
"Can you do it cheaper?"
Tell me what budget you are working with and I will tell you honestly what we can do within it. If the budget is below what our gear and crew costs to put together, I will say so. There is no point quoting you something that does not match what you have. What is the number you are working with?
Hormozi: always ask for the number before you move the price. Most people asking for cheaper do not actually know what cheaper means. Making them name the number either reveals a workable budget or ends the conversation cleanly.
"I need to check with my team / boss."
Of course. What does your team typically need to see to make a call on something like this? A quote, a portfolio, references? I want to make sure whatever I send you is useful for that conversation and not just more stuff for them to read. And who else is involved in the decision? Is it worth looping them in directly so I can answer their questions?
Do not wait passively. Find out what the decision process looks like and offer to be useful inside it. Asking who else is involved prevents the deal from dying in an inbox you cannot reach.

Negotiation tactics

Hormozi: never negotiate against yourself. The person who names the number first often loses. These tactics are built on the principle that confidence in price signals confidence in value. Discounting without reason destroys perceived quality.

1
Never drop the price without getting something in return
If someone asks for a lower rate, the answer is never just yes. You give something, you take something. Hormozi: value is relative. If you lower the price without a trade, you tell them the original price was made up.
"I can look at adjusting the rate. If we do two shoot days instead of one, I can give you a better per-day number. Does that work for the upcoming schedule?" OR: "If you can commit to a monthly retainer rather than per shoot, I can bring the per-day rate down. What does your shoot frequency look like over the next three months?"
2
Use silence after you quote
After you give a price, stop talking. Most people fill silence by justifying their price, which signals insecurity. Let the number sit. If they have an objection, they will say it. If they are calculating, let them calculate.
[Quote the number. Stop. Look at them or wait on the call. Do not speak until they do.]
3
Anchor high, give one concession
If you expect to negotiate, start with the full package. When they push back, offer one specific concession: a different package configuration, not a straight discount. This preserves the value of the package while giving them something to feel like they won.
"The full day package at that rate includes crew. If budget is the constraint, we can do dry hire only for the camera and lenses, and you sort the operator separately. That brings the number down to [dry hire rate]. Which would work better for this shoot?"
4
The retainer close (turn a one-off into a relationship)
When someone is negotiating on a single shoot day, offer the retainer as the answer. The per-day rate drops, they feel like they won. Lensr gets recurring revenue. Both sides win.
"The per-shoot rate is what it is for a single booking. But if you are shooting more than twice a month, a retainer brings the per-day number down significantly and also guarantees your availability. Do you want me to put together what that looks like for your typical shoot frequency?"
5
Counter the "we got a cheaper quote" move
Someone will say another vendor quoted them less. The worst response is to match it. The right response is to make them think about what they are actually comparing.
"I believe you. Can I ask what was in that quote? Was it dry hire only, or did it include crew? And which camera bodies and lenses? Most quotes that come in lower are missing something: crew, a specific lens, or 24/7 availability. I want to make sure you are comparing the same thing before you make the call. What did the quote include exactly?"
6
The take-away close (use sparingly)
Hormozi: when someone is going back and forth but not deciding, introduce mild scarcity. Not fake scarcity. Real scarcity about your availability or a specific piece of kit.
"I want to flag that we have two other shoots that week that have already come in. The FX9 and the Cooke lens set are the most requested. If the date is important to you, I would need to confirm this booking today to hold the kit. Otherwise I cannot guarantee availability."
7
Know your walk-away number
Before every negotiation, know the minimum you will accept and why. If someone pushes below that, the answer is no. A bad client at a low rate is worse than no client. Hormozi: say no with confidence and it will come back to yes more often than you think.
"I appreciate you being direct about the budget. At that rate I cannot put together the package that will actually serve your shoot properly. I would rather not take the booking and have the shoot go badly than take it and have you unhappy with the output. If the budget changes, we are here."
